The internet connects everyone, but it doesn't expose everyone to the same risks.

A journalist investigating government corruption, a business owner managing financial operations, a software developer working with production servers, and someone using the internet for everyday activities all have different security requirements.

Yet, many people approach cybersecurity in the same way. They install an antivirus, use a VPN, enable two-factor authentication, and assume they are secure.

But is that enough?

The reality is that **cybersecurity is not a one-size-fits-all solution.** The security measures you need depend on who you are, what information you handle, who might want that information, and what they could do to obtain it.

This is where the concept of **Individual Threat Modeling** comes in.

In this article, we'll explore how to understand your personal threat level, identify potential threats, and build a security approach that matches your actual risks.

> **Note**: Having a higher threat level doesn't necessarily mean someone is actively targeting you. It means your circumstances, exposure, and potential consequences may require stronger security measures.

---

## What Is an Individual Threat Model?

A threat model is a way of understanding the security risks associated with a particular person, organization, system, or activity.

In personal cybersecurity, it helps answer five important questions:

- **Who might target me?** Hackers, scammers, competitors, stalkers, malicious insiders, or other actors.
- **Why might they target me?** Financial gain, personal information, reputation damage, surveillance, or access to sensitive systems.
- **What could they target?** Email accounts, social media, personal devices, financial information, private conversations, or professional data.
- **How might they target me?** Phishing, social engineering, malware, account takeover, physical access, or data leaks.
- **What would happen if they succeeded?** Financial loss, identity theft, exposure of confidential information, reputational damage, or personal safety risks.

Understanding these questions helps you choose security measures based on actual threats rather than blindly following generic cybersecurity advice.

## Why Does Everyone Have a Different Threat Level?

Not everyone has the same digital footprint, responsibilities, exposure, or potential adversaries.

Consider these examples:

- A regular internet user might primarily face phishing, account theft, and financial scams.
- A journalist might face targeted phishing, surveillance, account compromise, and attempts to expose confidential sources.
- A business owner might face financial fraud, corporate espionage, ransomware, and business email compromise.
- A developer might face attacks targeting source code, cloud credentials, production infrastructure, and software supply chains.
- An activist might face doxxing, account surveillance, harassment, and targeted attacks against their communications.

The important point is that **the same security setup cannot adequately address every person's threat model.** For some people, their security measures might be overkill for their actual threat level, while for others, their existing security might be inadequate.

There is also a trade-off between security and convenience. **The higher your security requirements, the more convenience you may have to sacrifice, and vice versa.** Additional security measures can introduce extra authentication steps, stricter access controls, and more complex workflows.

Someone handling confidential sources needs different protections from someone primarily concerned about protecting their online banking account.

## Understanding Different Individual Threat Levels

For simplicity, we can divide individual threat profiles into five illustrative levels. We'll also explore a separate category of privacy-conscious users who may choose stronger security measures regardless of their actual threat exposure.

> **Note** These levels are not official security classifications. They are a practical framework for understanding how personal circumstances influence cybersecurity requirements.

### Level 1: Low Exposure - Everyday Internet Users

This profile generally includes people who use the internet for:

- Social media and entertainment
- Online shopping
- Personal communication
- Banking and digital payments
- General browsing

Their primary threats usually include:

- Phishing emails and messages
- Fake websites and online scams
- Password reuse attacks
- Social media account takeovers
- Identity theft
- Malicious applications

**Recommended Security Measures:**

- Use a password manager and unique passwords.
- Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA).
- Keep your operating system and applications updated.
- Avoid downloading software from untrusted sources.
- Enable transaction alerts for banking applications.
- Review social media privacy settings.
- Maintain regular backups of important files.
- A VPN is optional but can help protect network traffic on untrusted public Wi-Fi.

The objective is to protect personal accounts, financial information, and everyday digital activities.

### Level 2: Moderate Exposure - Professionals and Developers

This profile includes software developers, freelancers, IT professionals, researchers, and people who handle valuable professional information.

Their digital footprint may include:

- Source code and development projects
- Client information
- Work-related email accounts
- Cloud services and infrastructure
- Access credentials and API keys
- Internal company documents

Their potential threats include:

- Targeted phishing
- Credential theft
- Malicious software
- Repository compromise
- API key exposure
- Supply chain attacks
- Unauthorized access to cloud environments

**Recommended Security Measures:**

- Use hardware security keys or phishing-resistant MFA where possible.
- Separate personal and professional accounts.
- Use encrypted storage for sensitive information.
- Never commit secrets or API keys to public repositories.
- Apply least-privilege access controls.
- Secure SSH keys and development environments.
- Use endpoint protection and firewall controls.
- Maintain encrypted and tested backups.
- Use a trustworthy VPN when working on untrusted networks or accessing private development infrastructure.

For developers, security should also extend to the systems they build and maintain.

### Level 3: Elevated Exposure - Business Owners and Public-Facing Professionals

This category includes business owners, executives, financial professionals, consultants, and people with significant public or commercial exposure.

They may have access to:

- Financial records
- Customer databases
- Business email accounts
- Confidential contracts
- Internal communications
- Company infrastructure

Potential threats include:

- Business email compromise (BEC)
- Financial fraud
- Ransomware
- Corporate espionage
- Targeted social engineering
- Impersonation attacks
- Data exfiltration

**Recommended Security Measures:**

- Use phishing-resistant MFA for critical accounts.
- Implement role-based access control.
- Use separate administrative accounts.
- Encrypt sensitive business information.
- Monitor account activity and security logs.
- Establish an incident response plan.
- Train employees to recognize social engineering.
- Maintain offline or immutable backups.
- Secure domain registration and business email infrastructure.
- Consider a business VPN or Zero Trust network access solution to secure remote access to company resources.

At this level, a compromised personal account can potentially become an entry point into an entire organization.

### Level 4: High Exposure - Journalists, Activists, and Investigators

Journalists, investigative researchers, human rights defenders, and activists can face threats that go beyond ordinary financial cybercrime.

Their work may involve sensitive information, confidential sources, controversial subjects, or powerful individuals and organizations.

Potential threats include:

- Targeted phishing and account compromise
- Spyware and device surveillance
- Exposure of confidential sources
- Doxxing and online harassment
- Account impersonation
- Location tracking
- Unauthorized access to sensitive documents
- Attempts to compromise communication channels

**Recommended Security Measures:**

- Use end-to-end encrypted communication where appropriate.
- Prefer phishing-resistant authentication.
- Keep sensitive source information separate from everyday accounts.
- Use full-disk encryption on devices.
- Disable unnecessary location sharing and metadata exposure.
- Establish secure document-sharing procedures.
- Keep devices and applications updated.
- Use separate devices or environments for particularly sensitive work when justified.
- Develop a plan for device loss, account compromise, and source exposure.
- A trustworthy VPN can add a layer of network privacy, especially when working on untrusted networks.

For journalists, protecting information is not just about protecting themselves. It can also mean protecting their sources and the people involved in their investigations.

### Level 5: Very High Exposure - High-Value or Specifically Targeted Individuals

This profile may include individuals who face credible, specific, and potentially sophisticated threats.

Examples can include:

- People investigating highly sensitive matters.
- Individuals handling exceptionally valuable confidential information.
- People facing credible stalking or surveillance threats.
- Executives or researchers targeted by sophisticated adversaries.
- Individuals whose devices or communications could expose other people's safety.

Potential threats may include:

- Advanced persistent threats (APTs)
- Highly targeted social engineering
- Sophisticated spyware
- Device compromise
- Account recovery attacks
- Physical surveillance
- Insider threats
- Coordinated digital and physical attacks

**Recommended Security Measures:**

- Conduct a professional security assessment.
- Use a dedicated threat model for sensitive activities.
- Consider high-security devices and hardened operating environments.
- Use hardware-backed, phishing-resistant authentication.
- Minimize sensitive data stored on everyday devices.
- Establish strict account recovery procedures.
- Implement network and endpoint monitoring where appropriate.
- Develop incident response and recovery procedures.
- Seek specialist digital security support when credible threats exist.
- VPN usage should be part of a professionally assessed network-security strategy.

At this level, generic security recommendations are often insufficient. Security decisions should be based on the specific adversary, capabilities, and potential consequences.


## Beyond Threat Levels: Privacy-Conscious Users - Low Targeting Risk, High Security Posture

Not everyone who uses advanced security measures is necessarily a high-value target.

Some people simply value their privacy, digital independence, and control over their personal information. They may not have any known adversaries specifically targeting them, but they still prefer to maintain a strong security posture.

For example, someone might be an ordinary internet user and an open-source developer who deliberately chooses privacy-focused technologies and security practices.

Their security setup might include:

- Using a hardened Linux distribution as their primary operating system.
- Preferring open-source software and applications from trusted sources.
- Using a VPN for everyday internet connectivity.
- Enabling full-disk encryption and encrypting external storage drives.
- Using hardware security keys for account authentication.
- Using a password manager with unique, strong passwords.
- Minimizing unnecessary application permissions and background services.
- Keeping their operating system and software regularly updated.
- Limiting the amount of personal information shared online.

**Why do they need this level of security?**

Not necessarily because someone is actively targeting them, but because they believe privacy should be a default rather than something reserved for people with something to hide.

Their priorities might include:

- Reducing unnecessary data collection and tracking.
- Protecting personal files if a device is lost or stolen.
- Maintaining control over their digital environment.
- Reducing dependence on proprietary software and services.
- Preventing account compromise and unauthorized access.
- Minimizing their overall digital footprint.

**An Important Distinction:**

A person can have a relatively low likelihood of being specifically targeted while maintaining a very high personal security posture.

This is an important distinction between **threat level and security preference**. Your security setup doesn't always have to reflect the minimum protection required by your current threat level. You can choose stronger protections simply because privacy and security are important to you.

However, additional security also introduces trade-offs, including maintenance, compatibility issues, authentication steps, and reduced convenience.

The goal is to understand those trade-offs and choose a security setup that works for your everyday life.

## How to Determine Your Own Threat Level

You don't need to be a cybersecurity expert to start evaluating your personal threat model.

Begin by understanding your exposure, the information you handle, and the potential consequences of a compromise.

### Step 1: Identify Your Digital Assets

Make a list of the information and systems you need to protect.

Examples:

- Personal email accounts
- Banking and financial applications
- Social media accounts
- Personal photographs and documents
- Work-related information
- Cloud storage
- Passwords and authentication credentials
- Private conversations

Not all assets have the same value. Your primary email account, for example, may be especially important because it can be used to reset passwords for other accounts.

### Step 2: Identify Potential Threat Actors

Think about who could realistically want access to your information.

Examples:

- Cybercriminals looking for financial gain
- Scammers attempting identity theft
- Competitors seeking confidential business information
- Individuals involved in personal disputes
- People attempting to expose private information
- Sophisticated adversaries targeting sensitive professional work

Avoid assuming that every possible attacker is equally relevant. Focus on credible scenarios.

### Step 3: Understand Your Exposure

Consider how much information about you is publicly available.

Check:

- What personal information appears in search results?
- Are your email addresses publicly visible?
- Do your social media accounts reveal your location?
- Do you publish information about your daily routine?
- Have you exposed work-related information online?
- Are your professional relationships publicly identifiable?

Your public digital footprint can influence the ways someone might target you.

### Step 4: Evaluate the Potential Impact

Ask yourself what could happen if someone gained access to your accounts or devices.

For example:

- Would losing your email account affect other accounts?
- Could financial information be misused?
- Could confidential work be exposed?
- Could someone identify your private contacts?
- Could compromised information put another person at risk?

The potential impact matters just as much as the likelihood of an attack.

### Step 5: Choose Appropriate Security Measures

Once you understand your risks, select security measures that address them.

You don't need every available security tool. You need the right controls for your specific circumstances.

## Security Is Not About Using More Tools

One common misconception is that installing more security applications automatically makes someone safer.

That isn't necessarily true.

Using five different VPN services, multiple antivirus applications, and several privacy tools doesn't automatically create a stronger security posture.

In some situations, unnecessary tools can introduce additional complexity and configuration mistakes.

A better approach is to build security around your actual threat model.

For example:

- An everyday user may benefit more from a password manager and MFA than from an advanced network monitoring setup.
- A developer should prioritize credential management, secure development practices, and infrastructure access controls.
- A journalist handling sensitive sources may need secure communication, device encryption, and a carefully planned operational security (OPSEC) process.
- A specifically targeted individual may need professional security support rather than another consumer security application.

**The goal is not to achieve maximum security at any cost. The goal is to achieve appropriate security for your level of risk.**

## Common Mistakes in Personal Cybersecurity

### Using the Same Security Setup for Everything

Different accounts, devices, and activities have different security requirements.

**Fix:** Prioritize critical accounts and separate sensitive activities where necessary.

### Assuming a VPN Makes You Anonymous

A VPN encrypts traffic between your device and the VPN provider, but it doesn't prevent phishing, malware, account tracking, or identification through other means.

**Fix:** Understand what a VPN protects and combine it with other relevant security controls.

### Ignoring Physical Security

Digital security also depends on who can physically access your devices.

**Fix:** Use device locks, full-disk encryption, automatic locking, and secure device storage.

### Sharing Too Much Information Online

Publicly sharing personal information can make social engineering and impersonation easier.

**Fix:** Review privacy settings and avoid unnecessarily exposing personal details.

### Overestimating Your Security

Having antivirus software and MFA doesn't mean every possible attack is covered.

**Fix:** Regularly review your threat model, recovery options, account security, and backup procedures.

## Final Thoughts

Cybersecurity should start with understanding the person, not choosing the security tools.

A journalist protecting confidential sources, a developer securing production infrastructure, a business owner protecting customer information, and an everyday internet user safeguarding personal accounts all have different security requirements.

Even someone who isn't a high-value target might choose a much stronger security setup simply because they value privacy, digital independence, and control over their personal information.

There is no universal security setup that works equally well for everyone.

Start by understanding:

- What information you need to protect.
- Who might realistically want access to it.
- How they could attempt to obtain it.
- What the consequences of compromise would be.
- Which security measures can reduce those risks.
- How much convenience you're willing to sacrifice for additional security.

Your threat model can also change over time. A new job, public exposure, sensitive investigation, business responsibility, or personal situation can introduce new threats and require a different security approach.

Remember that **threat level and security preference are two different things.** You might have a relatively low threat level but still prefer hardened operating systems, encrypted storage, hardware security keys, and privacy-focused software.

**Don't build your security around what everyone else is using. Build it around what you actually need to protect and what you're comfortable maintaining.**

Because good cybersecurity isn't about having the most security tools or making everything as restrictive as possible. It's about understanding your risks, finding the right balance between security and convenience, and making informed decisions about your digital life.
